      How to read this

      What this is. Comparable companies analysis values a company by looking at what the market pays for similar businesses, expressed as multiples of revenue or earnings. If peers trade at 12× EBITDA, the target is worth roughly 12× its own EBITDA.

      Equity value vs. enterprise value. Equity value is what the shares are worth: price × diluted shares. Enterprise value is what the whole business is worth regardless of how it is financed: equity value plus debt, minus cash, because an acquirer would assume the debt and get the cash. EV is what you compare across companies with different leverage.

      Numerator and denominator must match. EV pairs with EBITDA, EBIT, and revenue, figures available to all capital providers. Price pairs with EPS, which is after interest and belongs to shareholders only. EV over net income is meaningless.

      LTM. Last twelve months, not the last fiscal year. It keeps every company on a current and comparable footing.

      NM. Not meaningful. A multiple with a negative or near-zero denominator is arithmetic without economic content, so it is excluded from the statistics rather than allowed to distort the median.

      Reading the implied range. Peer multiples applied back to the target produce a range, not a price. Low and high are the 25th and 75th percentiles of the peer set; mid is the median. The football field draws the same ranges as bars against a line at the current share price: a bar the line passes through is a methodology on which the stock trades in line with its peers, a bar wholly to the right says the peers imply more than the market pays, and to the left less.

      Where the candidates come from. Two sources, combined rather than ranked. Comp peers: the compensation peer group in the target's latest proxy statement, the names its board benchmarks executive pay against. Boards pick those for revenue scale and competition for talent, not business similarity (Nike lists Microsoft, Cisco, and Kimberly-Clark, and none of adidas, Puma, or Deckers), so the list is filtered first: a name is kept only if it shares the target's sector and its LTM EBITDA margin is within 50% of the target's (a 9.9% target admits 5.0%–14.9%), and every name removed is listed with its reason and can be added back. Screen: companies Yahoo files in the target's industry at a comparable size. Yahoo's industries are coarse, so a screen match is a candidate, not a peer. Each candidate is tagged with the source(s) that named it; a name in both is the strongest signal the tool can give and is listed first.

      The caveat. Neither source is a peer set. Comp peer groups reflect pay benchmarking; industry screens are too coarse. The output is only as good as the peer set, and choosing peers is judgment: remove and add names before trusting the numbers.
